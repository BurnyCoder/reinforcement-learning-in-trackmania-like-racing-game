## Corrections to apply

Corrections 1–7 change wording that is wrong or unsupported. Clarifications 8–11 add precision to claims that are confirmed.

1. **Section 5, Kirk et al.** Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.09794 (Sec. 4.2, 4.3, 6.3, 7).
   - Original: "For procedurally generated environments they recommend training on a set of seeds and testing on held-out seeds, reporting several test sets (one per kind of generalisation, including extrapolation along controllable factors) and several training-set sizes."
   - Replacement: "For purely procedurally generated environments they note that the only free choice is how many seeds to train on and that the only meaningful test set is the full seed distribution, which held-out seeds approximate; training on almost all seeds and testing on held-out ones tests generalisation only in a weak sense (not memorising). They therefore recommend combining procedural generation with controllable factors, reporting several test sets (one per kind of generalisation, including extrapolation along controllable factors), and evaluating several training-set sizes."

2. **Section 5, racing summary.** Sources: https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02439 (Table 3), https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.04909, https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.07971, https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.11575.
   - Original: "unseen-track results are rare and weak before Green et al. 2026: Fuchs' agent got stuck, Learn-to-Race dropped from 31.1 % to 11.8 %, only Dreamer transferred in Brunnbauer et al."
   - Replacement: "unseen-track results before Green et al. 2026 are few and mixed: Fuchs' agent got stuck in a hairpin on a different track, the Learn-to-Race SAC baseline completed 31.1 % of its training track and 11.8 % of the unseen test track, and in Brunnbauer et al. only Dreamer transferred; the positive cases are that Dreamer agent and robust PLR on CarRacing (above)."

3. **Section 6, Rudin et al.** Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.11978 (Sec. 2.2.1, App. A.1.1, Table 3).
   - Original: "fewer than about 25 steps per environment hurt."
   - Replacement: "on their task, controlled at 50 Hz, fewer than 25 consecutive steps per environment (0.5 s of simulated time) hurt, although their own configuration uses 24."

4. **Section 6, PQN.** Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.04811 (Sec. 4).
   - Original: "parallel Q-learning with layer normalisation and λ-returns, no target network or large replay buffer"
   - Replacement: "the parallelised Q-network: Q-learning over vectorised environments with layer normalisation and λ-returns, no target network or large replay buffer"

5. **Section 7, Colas et al.** Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06979 (Sec. 4.3, 6).
   - Original: "about 20 seeds give power 0.8 for an effect size of 1."
   - Replacement: "about 20 seeds per algorithm give power 0.8 for a relative effect size of 1 (a difference in means of one pooled standard deviation), and about 100 for 0.5 (Colas et al. 2019)."

6. **Section 7, rliable.** Sources: https://github.com/google-research/rliable (banner), https://pypi.org/pypi/rliable/json, own install test of 2026-11-14.
   - Original: "(Apache-2.0; the repository was archived in October 2025 but version 1.2.0 installs)"
   - Replacement: "(Apache-2.0; the repository was archived on 15 October 2025; version 1.2.0 still installs, but in our test with Python 3.12 its import fails with pandas 3.1.1 and works with `pandas<3`)"

## Research-practice checklist

Each item applies to this project: PPO on procedurally generated tracks, tests on held-out tracks, baselines, controls and ablations over several seeds, preregistration first.

1. Write each hypothesis with the result that would show it false, and design each experiment so its outcome differs between the competing hypotheses (Nanda, "How to Write ML Papers", "Good experiments distinguish between hypotheses").
2. Mark every analysis as specified before or after seeing results, because post-hoc analysis is weaker evidence (Nanda, "How to Write ML Papers", "Track pre/post-hoc analysis").
3. Fix the train, validation and test track seeds at the start and use the test tracks once, for the final model (Lones Sec. 3.1 "Don't allow test data to leak into the training process" and Sec. 4.5).
4. Choose checkpoints, hyperparameters and stopping points on validation tracks only, since repeated looks at one test set overfit it (Lones Sec. 4.3 "Do avoid sequential overfitting").
5. Compute observation and reward normalisation statistics from training rollouts only and freeze them for evaluation (Lones Sec. 3.1: scaling statistics "should be calculated using only the training data").
6. Report more than one test set, because a test set from the training generator shares its biases (Lones Sec. 4.1; Kirk et al. Sec. 6.3).
7. Train every configuration with several seeds and report the mean with a spread or 95 % interval (Lones Sec. 4.4 "Do evaluate a model multiple times").
8. Say what each error bar captures (training seed, test-track sample, evaluation episodes) and how it was computed (NeurIPS checklist item 7, "Experiment Statistical Significance").
9. Use at least 10 seeds for the main comparison and treat 5 as too few for a strong claim (Patterson et al. Sec. 2 take-away list, item 5; Agarwal et al. Sec. 4.1: percentile intervals are sound from N = 10).
10. Give each baseline the same tuning effort as the main method (Lones Sec. 5.1: "optimise each one to the same degree"; Patterson et al. Sec. 3.2, "Picking hyperparameter sets fairly").
11. Use the strongest available baselines, including simple scripted ones that might win (Nanda post 1: "beautiful methods that lose to dumb baselines"; Lones Sec. 5.2).
12. Compare methods with a statistical test and correct for the number of comparisons across ablations (Lones Sec. 5.3 and 5.4).
13. Compare agents on the same test tracks and seeds so that paired differences can be analysed (Patterson et al. Sec. 4.4).
14. Ablate one change at a time so that the source of a gain is identified (Lipton & Steinhardt Sec. 3.2, "Failure to Identify the Sources of Empirical Gains").
15. Label untested mechanisms as speculation, separate from explanations backed by an experiment (Lipton & Steinhardt Sec. 3.1).
16. Make the abstract's claims match the scope of the results, and do not generalise beyond the tested tracks and simulator (NeurIPS checklist item "Claims"; Lones Sec. 6.3 "Don't generalise beyond the data").
17. Include a limitations section that states assumptions and the number of runs behind each claim (NeurIPS checklist item "Limitations").
18. Report negative results and failed ideas next to the positive ones (Nanda post 2, "Truth-Seeking").
19. Watch replays of the trained policies, sampled at random rather than hand-picked, to catch exploits that metrics hide (Lones Sec. 6.5 "Do look at your models"; Nanda post 2 on implicit cherry-picking).
20. Release code, configurations, seeds and checkpoints, and keep a dated notebook of every run and decision (NeurIPS checklist item 4 "Experimental Result Reproducibility"; Schulman, "Keep a Notebook, and Review It").

## Missed in the review

1. **Two independent 3-way action heads in PPO.** The reference code is CleanRL's `ppo_multidiscrete.py` ([file](https://github.com/vwxyzjn/ppo-implementation-details/blob/main/ppo_multidiscrete.py), lines 124–140): one linear layer outputs `nvec.sum()` logits, they are split per head, each head is a `Categorical`, and the joint log-probability and entropy are the sums over heads (`logprob.sum(0)`, `entropy.sum(0)`). The Isaac Gym file the review builds on asserts a `Box` action space (line 250) and uses a `Normal` policy, so this part must be replaced. PQN needs a single `Discrete` space, so it will use one 9-way head; a 9-way PPO head is therefore a needed ablation to separate the algorithm from the action parameterisation.
2. **How Rudin et al. bootstrap on time-outs.** They add the discounted value to the reward at the time-out step. In their code ([rsl_rl `ppo.py`](https://github.com/leggedrobotics/rsl_rl/blob/main/rsl_rl/algorithms/ppo.py), lines 161–166): `rewards += gamma * values * time_outs`, where `values` is the critic's output for the observation the action was taken from, not for the post-reset observation. Appendix A.2 reports about 10–20 % higher total reward with it. Both CleanRL files treat a truncation as a termination (`ppo_continuous_action_isaacgym.py` line 311, `pqn.py` line 193), so both need this change, and the environment must report time-limit and no-progress cut-offs separately from finishes and crashes.
3. **Rudin et al. settings the review leaves out.** Entropy coefficient 0.01, clip 0.2, desired KL 0.01, learning rate multiplied or divided by 1.5 when KL leaves [0.5, 2] × target and bounded to [1e-5, 1e-2] (Table 3, Algorithm 1). CleanRL's default entropy coefficient is 0.0 (line 93), which suits Gaussian policies; categorical heads need a non-zero value chosen in the tuning sweep. Every PPO row of Makoviychuk et al. Table 17 uses minibatches of 16,384 or more.
4. **Quirks of the CleanRL Isaac Gym file.** `reward_scaler` is declared (line 101) and never used; learning-rate annealing and value-loss clipping are off; advantages are normalised per minibatch; episode returns are logged only for episodes that end in the first three steps of a rollout (line 291), so its return curve is a subsample and cannot serve as the evaluation.
5. **PQN has not been shown at thousands of environments.** The paper's own settings are 128 environments × 32 steps for Atari (2 epochs, 32 minibatches, learning rate 2.5e-4, λ 0.65, `EPS_START` 1.0, `EPS_FINISH` 0.001, `EPS_DECAY` 0.1, gradient norm 10) and 1,024 × 128 for Craftax (Appendix E, Tables 4 and 5); its environment-count ablation stops at 512 (Fig. 10). CleanRL's `pqn.py` defaults to 4 environments (line 43). A PQN baseline at 4,096 environments is outside the tested range and needs the same number of tuned configurations as PPO (Patterson et al.), or it is an untuned baseline.
6. **Seeds, interval and test.** Agarwal et al. find percentile stratified-bootstrap intervals reliable from about 10 runs and too narrow at 3 (Fig. 6). Colas et al. 2019 need about 20 seeds per algorithm for a one-standard-deviation difference, about 100 for half of one, and warn against the bootstrap as a significance test below 50 samples. A consistent plan is: at least 10 seeds for the main comparison, interquartile mean with a 95 % percentile stratified-bootstrap interval for reporting, Welch's t-test for the preregistered comparison, and paired differences on shared test tracks (Patterson et al. Sec. 4.4).
7. **What held-out seeds can show.** Kirk et al. state that training on nearly the whole seed space and testing on held-out seeds "does not test for zero-shot generalisation beyond avoiding memorising" (Sec. 4.2). With 50,000 training tracks the main test is of this kind, so the stronger claims rest on the extrapolation set, the hand-made tracks and the training-set-size sweep. Cobbe et al. place the closing of the gap near 10,000 levels and still see overfitting at 16,000, so the sweep (1, 10, 100, 1,000, 50,000) has no point in the range where the change is expected; 10,000 should be added.
8. **Episode length on out-of-distribution tracks.** Robust PLR's F1 tracks needed 1,500 or 2,000 steps against 1,000 for training tracks (App. D.2 of https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02439). The hand-made and extrapolation tracks here need time limits scaled to track length, or failures from running out of time will be counted as failures to generalise.
