### Learnings before the first training run (stages A and B)

These notes record what was tried, what went wrong and why, and how it was fixed, in the order it happened. Numbers quoted here are in the result files named next to them.

**Reusing tested physics instead of writing our own.** The first plan was a custom simulator validated by hand-derived physics tests. During planning the decision changed to reusing cannon-es's `RaycastVehicle` (MIT), already used by shipped browser racing games, and porting it line by line to PyTorch. Testing then becomes comparison with the original: the Node.js harness (`tests/js/parity_harness.mjs`) runs the game's own code, and the port must reproduce it. Mirroring every floating-point operation in cannon-es's order (vector normalisation multiplies by `1/n`, matrix products summed left to right, impulses applied wheel by wheel) made the port agree to within a few units in the last place: see `results/parity.json`. Two cannon-es details found while reading the source mattered for the port: the wheel's friction axle comes from the wheel's own transform, so its spin angle would be hidden state unless the axle is the rotation axis (it is in our setup); and `maxSuspensionForce` caps only the suspension impulse, not the force used for the grip limit.

**Simplifications forced by the engine.** cannon-es has no per-surface grip, so the planned low-grip road shoulder was dropped; only wheel rays touch the track, so a tipped car falls through and is caught by the "3 m below the road" rule instead of a separate roll-over rule. cannon-es also has no rolling resistance or engine model, so a speed-dependent engine force and a small coasting brake were added to the shared action-to-wheel mapping (`docs/formats.md`), identically in the game and the port.

**The eager simulator was launch-bound.** The first benchmark of the batched environment took about 280 ms per agent step at every batch size from 1,024 to 16,384 cars: the time went into launching thousands of tiny GPU kernels per tick, not into arithmetic. `torch.compile` in WSL2 fuses them; the compiled simulator is about ten times faster (`results/bench/`). Training therefore runs in WSL2, while publishing runs on Windows where the GitHub and Hugging Face logins are.

**Track generation hung silently in WSL.** The first full pipeline smoke test made no progress for 36 minutes. Cause: the generator's worker processes (Python's "spawn" start method) re-imported the main script and PyTorch, about 600 MB each; 28 of them exceeded WSL2's 15 GB, the kernel killed workers, and the process pool replaced them forever. A second copy left over from an app restart doubled the load. Fix: the track geometry moved to a PyTorch-free module, the script got a `__main__` guard, and the pool uses 8 processes.

**Hard landings.** Because the chassis never touches the road, a landing that pushes the suspension ray's origin below the road would let the car fall through. A drop test (now `tests/unit/test_physics_sanity.py::test_hard_landing_does_not_tunnel`) showed drops from up to 15 m (17 m/s vertical impact, more than the hardest generated jump landing) are absorbed and the car returns to its rest height, with the cannon-es default suspension damping of 10.
